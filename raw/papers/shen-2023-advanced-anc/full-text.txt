Summary
Active noise control (ANC) is becoming increasingly important in daily life with the growing desire for a quiet environment. ANC headphones are commonly used to reduce noise while working, studying, or relaxing. A traditional ANC headphone generates the anti-noise with reference microphones, error microphones, and secondary sources, which have the same amplitude but opposite phase as the noise. However, some practical challenges in ANC headphones must be overcome, such as adaptive algorithm instability and computation complexity consideration, dynamic noise reduction in the multi-noise environment, reference signal with low reference to interference ratio, and causality constraints. This thesis presents some algorithms and implementations for dealing with these difficulties and improving the noise reduction performance of ANC headphones.
An adaptive gain (AG) algorithm combining fixed filters with adaptive theory together with the multi-reference strategy is proposed in the thesis. The main motivation of the AG algorithm is to allow a larger step size to be applied compared to the FXLMS algorithm and result in faster convergence. Theoretical analysis of the proposed AG algorithm's step size bounds and convergence behavior is performed, and simulations and real-time experiments validate the AG algorithm's noise reduction performance.
The reference microphone is usually mounted on the earcup. It collects all the ambient noise surrounding the earcup, including some incoherent noise with the disturbance, thereby degrading the ANC system's noise reduction. In this thesis, the wireless microphones are placed in front of the noise sources we target to attenuate in the multi-noise environment to acquire the reference signal with a high reference-to-interference ratio. The look-ahead time provided by wireless transmission benefits the implementation of causality constraints in ANC. The thesis also proposes a wireless feedforward ANC with coherence-based selection to select the most coherent input signal to the ANC controller for the anti-noise generation. Furthermore, a multi-channel wireless ANC is extended with the coherence-based

weight determination algorithm to improve the noise reduction performance of wireless feedforward ANC.
One of the difficulties associated with wireless feedforward ANC is the requirement for prior knowledge of the reference signal. When an unexpected interference occurs in the environment not picked up by the reference microphone, the noise reduction capability of wireless feedforward ANC degrades dramatically. A hybrid ANC structure with an alternating switching method is proposed to address this practical issue. With the application of wireless techniques, this thesis also proposes a multi-channel wireless hybrid ANC with fixed-adaptive control selection to further enhance the noise reduction capability of the ANC headphone. Another possibility is to combine wireless and earcup microphones on the headphone. A module for error separation is used in conjunction with the wireless hybrid ANC to enhance convergence performance.

Introduction
1.1 Noise pollution
Noise pollution has become a global issue for several decades. Human physical and mental health will be impacted by unpleasant noise exceeding a certain threshold [1]. Different sound levels are acceptable for different situations. 75-90 decibel (dB) is permissible to protect the hearing and health of individuals. 45 - 60 dB is accepted to ensure conversation and communication. 35 - 50 dB is appropriate for sleeping and resting. The environmental noises are mainly: (a) traffic noise from automobiles, ships, subways, trains, airplanes, etc. Traffic noise has become the primary noise in cities due to the rapid increase of motor vehicles. (b) industry noise from a variety of industrial equipment. Industrial noise has a high sound level, substantially influencing workers and residents. (c) construction noise from heavy equipment on the construction site. (d) community noise from people's social activities, household appliances, and audio equipment [2, 3].
1.2 Passive noise control
Passive noise control (PNC) is one method to reduce noise. Conventional PNC technology focuses primarily on reducing source intensity, isolating the sound transmission path, and sound absorption [4, 5]. Designing quiet equipment, repairing,

and replacing older or hazardous equipment with advanced technologies are common strategies to reduce noise at its source [6]. Sound insulation enclosures and barriers are two viable approaches for isolating sound transmission paths. Noise barriers are commonly used to isolate noise from the roadway, railway, and industrial sources [7, 8]. It is also possible to build sound-dampening enclosures for loud equipment and isolation chambers from which operators can remotely control equipment. Sound absorption materials reduce noise by allowing sound waves to travel through porous materials and converting sound energy into heat due to friction and resistance. Common sound absorption materials include decoupled lead-based tiles, open-cell foams, and fiberglass.
Generally, only medium and high-frequency noise can be reduced with conventional PNC techniques. Certain PNC methods can reduce low-frequency noise, which only focuses on specific frequency bands, requiring costly and bulky mufflers. In contrast, the active noise control method is more effective at low-frequency noise cancellation.

1.3 Active noise control
Compared with PNC, active noise control (ANC) has received more attention as an effective method to reduce low-frequency noise, and it has shown great promise in recent years [9, 10]. The basic principle of ANC is to generate an anti-noise through a secondary source with the same frequency and a fixed phase difference with the primary (unwanted) noise [11, 12]. The superposition of primary noise and antinoise produces destructive interference for both of them, as illustrated in Fig. 1.1. In the ANC system, several technical terms are defined [13, 14]. The noise to be controlled in the ANC system is referred to as the primary noise. The anti-noise is generated and combined with the primary noise, thus resulting in the cancellation of both noises [15, 16]. The reference microphones (represented as R in Fig. 1.1) are positioned to pick up noises as the reference signals. The error microphones (shown as E in Fig. 1.1) are arranged to acquire the residual error signals as the adaptive controller's inputs. The secondary path refers to the transmission path between the secondary source (represented as S in Fig. 1.1) and the error microphone, which is modeled online or offline during the noise reduction process [17, 18]. Due to its high low-frequency noise cancellation efficiency, the ANC technique has been

1.4 ANC headphone
Traditional noise-canceling headphones, such as passive earmuffs, are intended to cover the user's ears for noise reduction. The passive earcup is effectively used to block external noise. The problems with closed-back and in-ear headphones are their weight and tendency to exert more acoustic pressure or acoustic occlusion effect on the user, preventing long-duration listening. Another consideration for earmuffs is that they decrease all incoming sounds by about the same level. For example, it is challenging to communicate when wearing earmuffs because they block off speech, making communication sound muffled or incoherent.

1.5 Motivation
The thesis is motivated by the excellent noise reduction performance of commercial ANC headphones due to the high reference-to-interference ratio provided by reference microphone arrays and the high computation load of ANC chips. However, current adaptive algorithms is unstable and have poor convergence speeds, making them difficult to implement in commercial products. Therefore, the primary focus of this thesis is to propose novel algorithms that can reduce computational complexity while achieving higher noise reduction levels. Real-time experiments in a normal room are conducted to validate the proposed algorithms in this thesis.
Hybrid ANC structure that combines feedforward and feedback filters is a good solution for ANC headphones. However, the current processor must be fast enough

Additionally, the reference signal with a low reference-to-interference ratio is essential for noise reduction performance in ANC headphones. This thesis evaluates the efficacy and feasibility of using wireless reference microphones in ANC headphones. Moreover, algorithms are developed for single-channel and multi-channel wireless ANC to enhance performance in terms of noise reduction and computation complexity.
1.6 Main contributions of the thesis

8

1.6. Main contributions of the thesis

I. An adaptive gain method is proposed to solve the instability problem in the adaptive algorithm caused by step size bounds in ANC headphones. In this approach, the reference signals are fed into several pre-trained control filters, whose outputs are adaptively weighted to form the control signal. The proposed algorithm increases the step size bounds and maintains satisfactory noise reduction performance by combining fixed-filter technology with an adaptive mechanism. Furthermore, the numerical simulations and real-time experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of dealing with noise in real scenarios.
II. This thesis proposes an alternating switching hybrid ANC structure to attenuate the uncorrelated noise, which is not picked up by the reference microphone in the feedforward ANC. The influence of the uncorrelated interference on the weight error vector and mean square error is analyzed first, and an alternating switching mechanism is presented. Compared with the conventional hybrid ANC, the proposed system attenuates the uncorrelated noise with the separated error signal. This solution allows only one feedback and feedforward control filter to update the coefficients during the noise reduction process, thereby reducing computational complexity. Numerical simulations and real-time experiments demonstrate the effectiveness in different acoustic scenarios.
III.To obtain a high reference-to-interference ratio reference signal, wireless microphones are placed in front of the targeted noise sources that we aim to attenuate in a multi-noise environment. It replaces the conventional microphone (mounted on the earcup) to be close to the targeted noise source. A coherence-based selection algorithm selects the high-coherent reference signals from the multi-noise source environment, which is used as the input of the feedforward control filter. A multichannel wireless ANC is expanded and implemented with multiple control filters to update the coefficients separately with the related filtered references. Furthermore, a coherence-based weight determination algorithm is proposed to pre-weight each wireless reference signal instead of only selecting or discarding the reference signals. Subsequently, the control signal is generated by the selected and weighted reference signals, thus improving the ANC headphones' noise reduction performance.
IV. To further improve the noise reduction performance of wireless feedforward ANC, a multi-channel wireless hybrid ANC structure (WHANC) is proposed in this thesis. It applies multi-channel feedforward ANC to deal with wireless reference signals, and a feedback structure is used to mitigate interference at the error

2.1 Active noise control structure

2.3.1 Wireless signal transmission

In wireless transmission, signals can be modulated into regions of the spectrum with lower noise and interference and better propagation properties. The following describes the wireless transmission of acoustic signals: the signal is modulated at a high frequency to generate a radio frequency signal, which is then transmitted into the air via the antenna. The long-distance radio frequency signal is received and reverse-modulated at the transmitter. When the frequency of an electromagnetic wave is less than 100 kHz, the wave is absorbed by the earth's surface and can't be transmitted effectively. When an electromagnetic wave has a frequency of more than 100 kHz, it can propagate into the air and be reflected by the ionosphere at the outer edge of the atmosphere to produce a long-distance transmission. Without the use of cables, wireless signals can be delivered from one transmitter to multiple receivers. With the advancement of wireless technology, wireless communication is being used in all aspects of life.
For acoustic signals, wireless transmission [59] is divided into analog and digital transmission. The analog transmission modulates and transmits the acoustic signal on the microwave channel directly. The data transmitted in analog has almost no time delay and compression loss. Moreover, the hardware used to implement analog transmission is inexpensive. However, analog transmission has a low antiinterference capability and is easily affected by the surrounding environment.
Digital transmission encodes and compresses the acoustic signal before sending it to the microwave channel. Digital transmissions are more resistant to interference than analog transmissions. However, the time delay for the A/D and D/A for wireless signal transmission should be considered in digital transmission. Hence, we applied analog transmission for wireless ANC in this thesis.

Modulation is required before the targeted signal enters the transmission channel to enable efficient and regulated signal distribution. However, in the ANC, where the desired signal is actually the noise to be attenuated, we introduce a new metric called the "reference-to-interference ratio (RIR)", which is calculated as:

RI R = Preference Pinterf erence

(2.58)

where Preference and Pinterference are the power of the reference signal and interference collected by the microphone. The RIR is affected by the distance between the noise source and the microphone, as the amplitude of the reference signal decreases with distance and more interference is mixed with the reference during sound transmission. Therefore, the closer the microphone is to the noise source, the higher the RIR of the reference signal.

Adaptive gain applied on the fixed filter for active noise control
Feedforward ANC is widely implemented in headphones to attenuate noise. An adaptive algorithm, such as the FXLMS algorithm, is used to update the coefficients of the feedforward filter to minimize the error signal received at the ear. On the other hand, ANC headphones must account for control filter divergence during the adaptation process. The fixed-coefficient filter is a pre-trained solution stored in the noise-canceling processor due to its fast response and robustness. However, their noise reduction performance suffers when dealing with primary noises coming from different directions. Hence, we propose an adaptive gain (AG) algorithm [65] for the fixed filters with the multi-reference method in this chapter. The control signal is formed by adaptive weighting and summing the outputs of multiple fixed filters, which is pre-trained from primary noise with different directions of arrival. The proposed algorithm outperforms the conventional fixed filters in noise reduction performance by combining the adaptive algorithm with the fixed filter approach. A theoretical analysis of the proposed algorithm's step size bound and time constant demonstrates its robustness and fast convergence behavior. Furthermore, comparative simulations and real-time experiments with commercial ANC headphones and other adaptive algorithms show the efficacy of the AG algorithm in dealing with multiple noise sources in the actual scenario.

An adaptive gain (AG) algorithm for ANC headphones using fixed filters was proposed in this chapter. Alternating switching hybrid active noise control
With better performance on broadband noise attenuation than feedback structure, the feedforward structure is usually adopted to implement ANC in many practical applications, such as the ANC headphone. Nevertheless, if the reference microphone does not pick up the narrowband noise uncorrelated with the reference signal, the simple feedforward structure cannot attenuate this uncorrelated noise effectively.
This chapter proposes an alternating switching hybrid ANC (ASHANC) [71]. Compared with the conventional hybrid ANC (HANC), the proposed algorithm can attenuate the uncorrelated noise with the separated error signal while reducing computation complexity. The mixed error signal is separated in this way. This solution allows only one of the feedforward and feedback filters to update the coefficients during the state of the noise reduction process, thereby reducing computational complexity. 4.2 Alternating switching hybrid ANC algorithm

HANC is widely used to deal with the problem mentioned in the previous section. The conventional HANC system is a combination of FF and FB filters. The FB filter in the hybrid ANC is able to reduce the uncorrelated narrowband noise, which is not picked up by the reference microphone. However, the error signal mixed with uncorrelated narrowband noise degrades the convergence speed. A practical solution to this problem is to exclude the uncorrelated noise from the error signal. One of the algorithms based on this strategy is the cascading hybrid ANC [58], which utilizes an additional adaptive filter to filter out the uncorrelated noise for the coefficients updating. Nevertheless, the additional adaptive filter increases the computational complexity of the algorithm and hence, undermines its practical application. Therefore, we propose the ASHANC to separate the mixed error signal.

In contrast to the conventional HANC structure, the proposed ASHANC algorithm possesses several "switches", as shown in Fig. 4.2. In order to separate the error signal, two states are defined to switch the updating of the FF and FB filters in different situations. In the algorithm, a state machine equipped with a slope calculator is used to control the state transfer, which is described in detail in the following section.

In State 1, the FB filter executes the coefficients updating based on the FXLMS algorithm, while the FF filter remains at its original value. During this state, the update equation is given by

  wb(n + 1) = wb(n) - µbxb(n)e(n),
 wf (n + 1) = wf (n).

(4.14)

Once the FB filter attenuates the uncorrelated narrowband noise to the minimum value, a trigger signal is generated to drive the state machine into State 2. In

  wf (n + 1) = wf (n) - µf xf (n)e(n),

4.2.1 The mechanism of state transformation

Following the previous discussion, an effective switching strategy should be derived to control the transfer of the state for ASHANC. Some existing methods have been proposed. One approach is to use the ratio of the power of secondary errors to the power of residual errors as the criterion to decide the switching of the states [73]. The ratio of the power of current residual errors to the power of a prior residual error is used to decide the reverse conversion. This method is suitable for the situation where the secondary path changes suddenly. Another switching strategy is based on the frequency-domain adaptive algorithm [74]. This method aims to make a switch between the bin-normalized frequency domain block least mean square (NFBLMS) algorithm (State 1) and the modified frequency domain block least mean square (MFBLMS) algorithm (State 2). It enables the MFBLMS algorithm after the NFBLMS algorithm reaches a steady-state and returns to the NFBLMS algorithm when microphones detect variations in the environment.

4.5 Conclusion
This chapter demonstrated that uncorrelated narrowband noise not picked up by the reference microphone degraded the convergence and noise reduction performance of the feedforward ANC. To solve this issue, we proposed an alternating switching hybrid active noise control algorithm (ASHANC), which automatically switched the updating of the feedforward and feedback filters to attenuate the uncorrelated narrowband noise. As proved in this chapter, this proposed algorithm reduced about 24%-26% computational complexity in terms of multiplications (additions) than the conventional HANC. Furthermore, simulations were conducted to validate the effectiveness of the ASHANC algorithm, whose noise reduction performance has been compared with the feedforward ANC, conventional hybrid ANC, and cascading hybrid ANC. These results confirmed that ASHANC had less computational complexity than the conventional hybrid ANC. The results of the experiment conducted in real-time indicated that ASHANC could reduce both the broadband noise and interference present in the environment and achieved a noise reduction level that was 4 - 8 dB better than the commercial headphone.

Wireless ANC utilizes the wireless microphone to replace the conventional microphone in the ANC system. The wireless microphones are placed close to the potential noise sources to acquire the reference signal with a high reference-tointerference ratio. The long-distance between the reference microphone and the secondary source avoids the feedback effect in the conventional FF ANC. Furthermore, the wireless transmission, which is much faster than the speed of sound in the air, provides adequate look-ahead time for the ANC control filter to compute the control signal. 5.1.2 Causality constraints investigation
The causality constraint is a significant problem to be solved in the ANC implementations [76]. To meet the causality constraints, the delay p for the noise signal traveling between the noise source and the internal error microphone in the headphones must be greater than the sum of the delay r for the noise signal transmitting from the noise source to the external reference microphone, the electronic delay e through the control system, and the delay s in the secondary path. The electronic delay e through the controller includes the delays associated with the

Chapter 5. Wireless feedforward ANC

91

Figure 5.3: The block diagram of time consumption during ANC process in the headphone.
A/D interface, the signal processing, and the electroacoustic conversions associated with reference microphones and secondary sources.
The advantage of the wireless ANC is the large look-ahead time by attaching the reference microphone to the noise source. For the conventional ANC headphones, the sound wave arrives at the earcup and is picked up by the reference microphone (latency: tre), as shown in Fig. 5.3. The analog signal is subsequently converted to a digital signal and passed to the ANC processor via wire transmission (latency: tad), and the ANC controller computes the anti-noise (latency: tco) and converts it to an analog signal (latency: tda). To meet the causality constraints, the total time delay (tre +tad +tco +tda) should be smaller than the actual sound wave transmitting from the noise source to the secondary source(latency: ts) [13]. Typically, the distance between the reference microphone and the secondary source is very small in the ANC headphone. Hence, the smaller the latency tre, the larger time allocation can be provided to the latency tco for the ANC controller computing the control signal, and the better the noise reduction that can be achieved. The wireless ANC shortens the latency tre by further placing reference microphones to pick up the reference signals earlier and forward them wirelessly. This 'look-ahead time' approach provides advanced information for the ANC to generate better matching anti-noise to the noise signal received at the headphones.

As introduced in the previous chapter, the application of the wireless technique improves the noise reduction performance of the ANC headphone. However, the wireless feedforward structure requires prior knowledge of the location of the noise sources. If interference occurs during the noise reduction procedure, wireless feedforward ANC cannot cope with the problem. The feedback structure, which employs only the error microphone to avoid the requirement of coherence between the reference and error signals, can solve this issue. A multi-channel wireless hybrid feedforward-feedback ANC structure (WHANC) [79] is proposed in Section 6.1. 6.2 Multi-channel wireless and earcup hybrid ANC with error separation module

This section describes a new hybrid approach that uses wireless microphones and the reference microphone on the earcup to pick up reference signals. Due to space constraints, a conventional ANC headphone typically has only one error microphone installed inside the earcup. As a result, the error signal is used to update the coefficients of multiple adaptive filters. Theoretical analysis reveals that the residual error components from other filters degrade the convergence performance of ANC [82]. Therefore, an adaptive filter cascaded with the noise control filter is introduced to separate signals at the error microphone. In the proposed hybrid approach, we extend the cascading adaptive algorithm to an error separation module (ESM) [50]. The ESM provides a separate error signal for each control filter that is implemented with an independent wireless reference signal, thus, improving the steady-state performance.

6.3 Conclusion
This chapter developed two kinds of hybrid structures in wireless ANC: a hybrid combination of wireless feedforward and feedback ANC and a hybrid combination of wireless feedforward and earcup microphone ANC.
Section 6.1 demonstrated the multi-channel wireless hybrid feedforward-feedback ANC with a fixed-adaptive control selection technique. Multi-channel feedforward structures were implemented to attenuate the wireless reference signals. A feedback control filter was used to reduce uncorrelated interference that occurred in the environment, which was not picked up by the wireless microphone. To further reduce the computation burden of wireless hybrid ANC structure, a fixed-adaptive control selection technique was employed in three states to determine whether the coefficients of feedforward and feedback control filters should be updated. Compared with the conventional hybrid ANC, the fixed-adaptive control selection technique reduced almost 32% computational complexity. Simulation and experiment results validated that the wireless hybrid ANC had 7.3 dB and 8 dB more noise reduction than the conventional hybrid ANC and the commercial ANC headphones, respectively, for multiple noise sources. In the case of uncorrelated interference, noise reduction using the proposed wireless hybrid structure was improved by 9.4 dB over the wireless feedforward ANC.
Section 6.2 developed a novel hybrid approach for ANC structure using wireless and earcup microphones to acquire the reference signal in a multi-source scenario. In

addition, an error separation module (ESM) was implemented to provide adaptive filters with individual error signals, which improved the noise reduction performance even more, as proved by numerical simulations. According to the real-time experiments, ESM improved by around 2dB compared to the one without ESM. The proposed wireless and earcup microphone hybrid ANC outperformed wireless feedforward ANC with 10 - 12 dB of noise reduction for interference. Moreover, the proposed method was effective for multiple noises, reducing noise by 6 dB more than the commercial ANC headphone.

Conclusion and Future work
7.1 Conclusion
The main objective of this thesis is to enhance the noise reduction capabilities of ANC headphones in environments with multiple noise sources without increasing the computation complexity. The adaptive feedforward filter implemented in the ANC headphones caused slow convergence and instability. In contrast, the fixed filter was only effective against certain noises from known directions and sources. We developed an adaptive gain approach to combine the benefits of fixed filters and adaptive algorithms. However, the adaptive gain technique had a problem in that it could not reduce noise that was not acquired by the reference microphone. These uncorrelated noises degraded the effectiveness of noise reduction. As a result, we proposed the hybrid ANC with an alternating switching algorithm that, in contrast to feedforward ANC, reduced uncorrelated noise without increasing computation complexity.
Nevertheless, adaptive gain and alternative switching hybrid ANC utilized the reference microphones on the earcups to receive mixed reference signals in a multisource situation. Mixed reference signals diminished the noise reduction capabilities of ANC headphones with a low reference-to-interference ratio. Therefore, we attempted to replace the conventional reference microphones in the ANC headphones with wireless microphones in front of noise sources. The wireless reference design reduced causality constraints and feedback effect due to the reference and error microphones' proximity to feedforward ANC. Furthermore, with this wireless

Various hybrid combinations (wireless feedforwardfeedback ANC with fixed-adaptive control selection and wireless/earcup microphone combinations with error separation module) were further proposed to improve ANC headphones' noise reduction capability.

7.2 Future work
The current trend in hearables is towards true wireless stereo (TWS) earbuds, which are easier to carry daily than headphones. One of the primary considerations in implementing the ANC feature is that it is substantially more challenging when using earphones. This is because earphones are made to fit in the ear canal, whose sizes vary from person to person and may result in different fitting degrees, whereas over-the-ear headphones form a perfect seal around the ear. Divergence in the

150

7.2. Future work

adaptive ANC algorithm may cause a high SPL noise level, resulting in a hearing loss since earphones are worn close to the eardrum. Consequently, we must put more effort into ensuring adaptive ANC stability in earphones.
Wireless ANC can selectively cancel out noise. By placing a wireless microphone in the appropriate location, we may manually eliminate annoying noise while maintaining the desired signal. Future work can include the evaluation of selective speech cancellation using wireless ANC headphones. For instance, we want to hear the presenter's speech clearly while suppressing the noise from our neighbors' conversations in a meeting.
The algorithms proposed in this thesis outperform commercial headphones in the mid-range (250Hz-2.5kHz) of the noise cancellation spectrum, but not as well as in the bass frequency range, according to the experiments conducted over the last few years. A possible approach to enhancing the bass frequency reduction is to include separate speaker drivers inside each earcup and channel different bands of anti-noise to each driver.
Finally, wireless reference ANC can be applied to open-ear hearables, such as a pair of wearable glasses with micro-speakers on the frame that emit anti-noise to the ear openings, with error microphones being placed close to the ear openings, without the problem of acoustic feedback from the speakers to the reference microphones. Alternatively, headrest speaker and shoulder speaker systems can potentially benefit by placing wireless reference microphones near the noise source and transmitting them to the headrest or shoulder units to perform the ANC feature in these systems. Several new experiments can be conducted with these ideas.
