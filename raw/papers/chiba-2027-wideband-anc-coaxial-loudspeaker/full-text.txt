Wideband active noise control for open-ear headphones via low-delay 
acoustic design with a coaxial loudspeaker
Hironobu Chiba∗, Noriyoshi Kamado, Tatsuya Kako, Kenichi Noguchi
NTT, Inc., Tokyo, Japan
H I G H L I G H T S
• A delay budget separates electronic, acoustic, and mechanical terms for wideband ANC.
• A coaxial woofer-tweeter source exploits complementary driver delay characteristics.
• Measured-path offline evaluation quantifies pink-noise reduction over 100–2000 Hz.

A B S T R A C T
Open-ear headphones preserve environmental awareness but provide little passive attenuation, so broadband 
feedforward active noise control (ANC) is required. This paper presents a controlled measured-path offline study 
of a wideband ANC design using one device in one dummy-head wearing configuration, based on a design-
oriented secondary-path delay budget. A coaxial offset 2-way secondary source with a tweeter placed in front of 
a woofer is used, and the control bands are assigned on the basis of frequency-dependent delay characteristics, 
where the woofer is favored at low frequencies because of its smaller mechanical delay, while the tweeter is 
favored at higher frequencies because of its shorter acoustic propagation delay. We investigated two implemen­
tations: one for a dual-filter setup and the other for a single-filter setup. In this idealized linear measured-path 
evaluation under controlled assumptions, the two-driver configurations increased band-limited noise-power re­
duction for broadband pink noise relative to the unfiltered single-driver baselines. At the nominal controlled 
virtual electronic delay of 104 µs, the background dual-filter condition Du-BpHp-Bg and the single-filter condi­
tion Xo-BpHp achieved 8.01 and 7.58 dB reductions from 100Hz to2000 Hz , respectively, compared with 5.94 dB 
for Wf-Bp and 5.02 dB for Tw-Bp. For the tested device and wearing configuration, these results indicate that 
a design-oriented delay budget can support driver-band allocation for broadband feedforward ANC in open-ear 
headphones. 
1 . Introduction
Open-ear headphones, which do not block the ear, have attracted 
increasing attention as wearable audio devices that allow simultaneous 
perception of reproduced audio and surrounding sounds [11]. Such de­
vices are promising for Audio Augmented Reality (Audio AR), where 
subjective evaluations have confirmed favorable perceptual impressions 
[34]. Moreover, physical measurements have shown that open-ear de­
signs achieve Pressure Division Ratio (PDR) values close to 0 dB with 
high acoustic transparency [ 4]. Although their openness inevitably 
causes sound leakage, recent enclosure designs based on opposite-phase 
radiation have mitigated this limitation [ 3], and high wearing comfort 
during prolonged use can be achieved through pinna-adapted device 
design [36].
The openness that gives these devices their advantages also makes 
them highly susceptible to environmental noise. Users therefore tend 
to increase playback volume to unsafe levels in noisy conditions, pos­
ing a risk of noise-induced hearing loss [ 35]. Accordingly, active noise 
control (ANC) is particularly important for open-ear headphones be­
cause it is effective at low frequencies, where many environmental 
noise sources contain substantial energy [ 6]. In headphone ANC, the 
short acoustic path between the noise source and the ear imposes a 
stringent causality constraint: when the electronic delay exceeds the

acoustic propagation delay, the system becomes non-causal and the 
noise reduction bandwidth is severely restricted [ 19,20]. Conventional 
feedback ANC addresses non-causality by adopting the internal model 
control (IMC) structure [ 5,7]; however, the inherent one-step predic­
tion in reference signal synthesis limits cancellation to predictable noise 
components, leaving broadband noise such as white noise theoreti­
cally intractable [ 17,33]. Accordingly, feedback ANC headsets have 
been primarily designed for low-frequency predictable noise [ 10], and 
a feedback ANC applied to lightweight open-type earphones demon­
strated a reduction only over 1000–3200 Hz for stationary, harmonically 
structured magnetic resonance imaging noise [18]. As an alternative ap­
proach under non-causal conditions, prediction-based ANC that utilizes 
the quasi-periodicity and harmonic structure of audio signals has been 
proposed [13]. However, its reliance on speech-specific spectral char­
acteristics precludes its application to broadband environmental noise. 
Therefore, a feedforward architecture that satisfies the causality con­
straint by design is indispensable for effective broadband noise control 
[14].
In feedforward ANC, the causality constraint requires that the sec­
ondary path delay not exceed the acoustic propagation delay of the 
primary path; violation of this condition degrades broadband cancella­
tion as the controllable bandwidth narrows with increasing non-causal 
delay [16]. This relationship has been confirmed for a single-channel 
feedforward ANC headset, where both the noise reduction bandwidth 
and maximum attenuation decrease with increasing non-causal delay 
[37]. A substantial body of work has focused on reducing electronic 
and algorithmic delays to satisfy this constraint. The latency of anti-
aliasing and reconstruction filters in conventional ADC/DAC chains has 
long been recognized as a significant delay contributor [ 14]. To elimi­
nate it, sigma-delta modulation-based architectures that operate directly 
on oversampled bitstream signals have been proposed [ 23,24]. Higher 
sampling rates and multirate techniques have been utilized to reduce 
per-sample delay [1,21,30,38]. Separately, the delayless subband archi­
tecture achieves both fast convergence and low latency by generating 
wideband anti-noise through a fullband filter while confining subband 
decomposition to a background update path [27,28].
While reducing electronic delay is essential, the total delay in head­
phone ANC is also determined by acoustic and mechanical components, 
both of which are comparably important. Acoustic delay, which is gov­
erned by the physical arrangement of microphones and loudspeakers, 
directly constrains causality. Several hardware-oriented strategies have 
been explored to relax this constraint, including the use of contralateral 
reference microphones in headphones to extend the effective primary-
path delay [2], multiple head-mounted reference microphones to satisfy 
causality for noise arriving from different directions [26], duct-like semi-
open enclosure structures that achieve a long and direction-independent 
primary path [ 29], and IoT-based relay nodes that provide additional 
acoustic lead time for the reference signal [ 31]. However, the total de­
lay is also influenced by mechanical delay in the transducers themselves. 
Digital PDM MEMS microphones exhibit group delay determined by 
the decimation filter, ranging from several hundred microseconds near 
100 Hz to around 10 µs above 1 kHz [15]. In addition, the loudspeaker 
group delay, although relatively underexplored in the ANC literature, 
can be a dominant component of the secondary-path delay. Because 
the mass–spring resonance of a loudspeaker concentrates group delay 
near and below the fundamental resonant frequency [ 25,32], compact 
loudspeakers with limited diaphragm area and enclosure volume gener­
ally have elevated fundamental resonant frequencies, often in the range 
of several hundred hertz, resulting in limited low-frequency radiation 
capability [22]. Moreover, their group delay inherently increases from 
around this frequency region, even within the low-frequency band. This 
issue is particularly critical in open-ear devices, which typically rely on 
compact drivers despite the fact that environmental noise often con­
tains substantial low-frequency energy, where ANC is most needed. 
Achieving acoustic transparency with a larger driver in open-ear de­
vices generally requires a larger source-to-ear distance, which in turn 
increases the acoustic delay of the secondary path. Although some ve­
hicle ANC systems utilize loudspeaker arrays that include both woofer 
and tweeter units, their main focus is spatial multi-channel control of 
low-order engine noise, not delay-oriented band allocation. For example, 
Ferrari et al. investigated engine-order cancellation up to 1 kHz using 
multiple car-audio transducers in a multi-channel FxLMS framework, 
but each secondary source operates over the same frequency range 
without explicit band-split control based on driver delay character­
istics [ 9]. To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has used a 
design-oriented secondary-path delay budget that separates acoustic 
propagation, mechanical group delay, and crossover-filter group delay 
to allocate different frequency bands to distinct drivers for broadband 
ANC.
In this paper, we focus on open-ear headphones and systematically 
revisit the delay components that limit broadband feedforward ANC, 
with emphasis on the acoustic and mechanical delays of the loudspeaker 
unit. We propose a coaxial loudspeaker unit comprising two drivers with 
distinct frequency ranges and delay characteristics, and partition the 
ANC control bandwidth so that each driver operates in the band where 
its delay is minimal. Because open-ear headphones generally cannot sat­
isfy the strict causality constraint over the full target bandwidth, we 
formulate the design objective as minimizing the secondary-path group 
delay to extend the frequency range over which partial noise reduction 
remains achievable. This motivation differs from that of a conventional 
audio 2-way crossover. Specifically, in many practical ANC applications, 
the main control objective is concentrated in the lower-frequency re­
gion, where a single low-frequency transducer is often sufficient and 
there is less need to exploit a tweeter while jointly optimizing acous­
tic and mechanical delays [ 6,10]. In contrast, the present open-ear 
target extends into a wider band for which passive attenuation does 
not relax the ANC requirement, so delay-oriented driver allocation be­
comes essential rather than merely acoustically convenient [ 29]. The 
proposed band-split coaxial architecture is designed to reduce the effec­
tive secondary-path delay relative to single-driver operation across the 
target bandwidth.

3 . Wideband ANC via delay-oriented coaxial loudspeaker 
configurations
3.1 . Coaxial offset 2-way secondary source
To overcome the delay trade-off identified in Section 2, we intro­
duce a coaxial offset 2-way secondary source comprising a woofer and 
a tweeter arranged coaxially, with the tweeter mounted in front of the 
woofer as shown in Fig. 2. In this arrangement, the tweeter is positioned 
closer to the error microphone than the woofer, so that its acoustic de­
lay 𝜏A
S,T is smaller than the woofer’s 𝜏A
S,W. At the same time, the woofer’s 
larger diaphragm yields a lower fundamental resonant frequency 𝑓0,W, 
resulting in smaller mechanical group delay 𝜏M

region where the tweeter’s mechanical delay 𝜏M

in isolation using a JIS standard enclosure to exclude acoustic propaga­
tion effects, confirm this complementary relationship and are presented 
in Section 4.2.
By allocating low-frequency control to the woofer and high-
frequency control to the tweeter, the effective secondary-path delay 
can be reduced across the target bandwidth. Although restricting each 
driver to its intended frequency range may reduce distortion risk in a 
physical implementation by avoiding inefficient out-of-band excitation, 
this potential nonlinear benefit is outside the scope of the present linear 
measured-path evaluation. The specific driver dimensions and distances 
for the device used in this study, together with the measured paths used 
in the offline evaluation, are described in Section 4.
To achieve this delay-selective band allocation, we consider two 
ways of placing the band-splitting filters in a two-driver ANC sys­
tem. First, in the dual-filter configuration, independent adaptive-filter 
branches are assigned to the woofer and tweeter, and the band-splitting 
filters may be placed either in the background update path or in the 
foreground output path. Second, in the single-filter configuration, one 
adaptive-filter branch is followed by a fixed crossover network and a 
gain-matching stage in the foreground path. These configurations are 
described in the following subsections.

and gradient-estimation errors. Moreover, where the secondary-path 
delay violates causality, the available phase relationship limits the at­
tainable cancellation and can lead to large filter coefficients or control 
outputs in frequency regions where the driver is inefficient. In a physi­
cal implementation, such outputs may increase nonlinear-distortion risk, 
which is not represented in the present linear evaluation.

3.4 . Configuration comparison

background dual-filter configuration, the band splitting acts only on the

model.

5 . Discussion
5.1 . Relationship between delay and noise reduction
The results support the delay-oriented interpretation introduced in 
Section 2, but this interpretation is not a point-by-point prediction of 
the measured attenuation. Within its stated phase-error range, Eq. (6) 
explains the basic trend that a fixed excess delay causes a phase error 
proportional to frequency and therefore reduces the relaxed causality 
margin more severely in the upper part of the control band. The delay 
sweep in Fig. 12, however, also showed pronounced degradation around 
500 Hz and 1 kHz. This observation is consistent with the isolated 
driver measurements in Fig. 7, since the compact headphone drivers 
have resonance-related mechanical delay in the low hundreds of hertz, 
this mechanical component appears close to the ANC control band and 
consumes the causality margin together with the electronic delay.
This is the main reason for introducing the design-oriented delay 
budget in Table 5. Reducing only the electronic delay is important, but it 
is not sufficient when the loudspeaker’s mechanical delay is located near 
the target control band. The woofer and tweeter provide complemen­
tary advantages in this respect: the woofer reduces the mechanical-delay 
burden in the low-frequency region, while the tweeter becomes useful 
at higher frequencies after its resonance-related delay decreases and its 
shorter acoustic propagation delay becomes beneficial. Thus, the pro­
posed 2-way allocation should be understood not as an audio crossover 
rule or an exact physical decomposition of a worn-device path, but as a 
way to reduce the design budget of the effective secondary-path delay 
under the strict causality constraint of open-ear feedforward ANC.
At the same time, the evidence for this interpretation is deliber­
ately separated. The isolated JIS-enclosure measurement in Fig. 7  is 
used to define the mechanical group-delay basis in Table 5, the geom­
etry in Fig. 6 is used to estimate the acoustic propagation delay, and 
the high-pass-filter design gives the crossover-filter group delay. The 
worn-device impulse and amplitude responses in Figs. 8 and 9 docu­
ment the measured paths utilized in the offline evaluation. Since the 
worn secondary paths include ear geometry and local reflections, their 
group delay is not decomposed into acoustic and mechanical terms. 
Similarly, Eq. (6) assumes a single-frequency sinusoid, nonnegative opti­
mal amplitude matching, and a phase error below the first 90◦ boundary, 
whereas the present evaluation uses finite-length adaptive filters, mea­
sured paths, and broadband stochastic excitation. At or beyond that 
boundary, Eq. (6) is not periodically extrapolated; the practical result 
may instead be no positive attenuation. The model therefore serves as 
an explanatory framework for the observed band dependence, not as a 
quantitative predictor of every value in Figs. 10 and 12.
5.2 . Architectural trade-offs and design guidance
The results also clarify how the two proposed implementations 
should be selected. From an output-path delay perspective, the back­
ground dual-filter configuration is structurally favorable because the 
band-limiting filters act only in the adaptation path and do not add 
group delay to the anti-noise output path. This advantage is most 
clearly isolated by the tweeter-only comparison: Tw-Hp-Bg outperforms 
Tw-Hp-Fg in the mid-frequency bands, demonstrating that placing the 
same high-pass filter in the foreground output path can consume a 
substantial part of the causality margin. In the dual-filter architec­
ture, however, the foreground/background difference is less systematic 
because the woofer path, the MSE-based allocation between the two 
adaptive-filter branches, the differences between the left and right 
paths, and the path interference all affect the final solution. Thus, 
the filter-placement effect is clear for the tweeter-only branch, but 
is path-dependent in the full dual-filter architecture. The background 
dual-filter placement should therefore be preferred primarily when pre­
serving the output-path causality margin is the dominant design priority, 
for example when a steeper band-limiting filter would otherwise in­
troduce non-negligible output-path group delay. Under the first-order 
filter used in the present evaluation, this structural delay advantage 
does not necessarily produce a larger noise reduction averaged over 
the left and right ears in every band or at every added electronic
delay.
The single-filter configuration offers a different compromise. It treats 
the woofer and tweeter as one composite secondary path controlled 
by a single adaptive-filter branch, thereby reducing the control-output 
convolution rate, coefficient-element commitment rate, and coefficient 
storage by approximately half compared with the dual-filter implemen­
tation, as summarized in Tables 1  and 2. The trade-off is that the 
crossover network is necessarily in the foreground output path, so its 
filter group delay contributes directly to the composite secondary-path 
delay. The comparison between Du-BpHp-Bg and Xo-BpHp therefore 
indicates a useful design trade-off rather than a simple winner: the dual-
filter configuration is slightly more favorable for attenuation, while the 
single-filter configuration retains much of the broadband benefit with 
lower foreground convolution and coefficient-storage cost.
The bypass/bypass conditions, Du-BpBp and Xo-BpBp, should be in­
terpreted as lower-delay references rather than practical optima. While 
they are useful for demonstrating what can be obtained when no band-
limiting filter delay is introduced, they do not suppress inefficient 
tweeter drive below its intended operating range. Among the tested 
conditions, the bypass/high-pass allocation is the more practical design 
choice: it preserves the woofer’s low-frequency role, keeps the tweeter 
away from its inefficient low-frequency region, and avoids adding un­
necessary filtering to the woofer branch. This design guidance is specific 
to the present drivers, geometry, and measured paths. A practical design 
sequence is to identify the frequency range in which each driver pro­
vides adequate amplitude response and lower mechanical group delay, 
include the source-to-ear acoustic offset and crossover-filter and elec­
tronic delays in the total delay budget, and then select the driver bands 
and filter placement according to the remaining causality margin. If the

output-path causality margin is the dominant constraint, background 
dual-filter placement is structurally favorable; if computational load is 
dominant and the crossover-filter delay is acceptable, the single-filter

5.3 . Limitations and scope
Several limitations define the scope of the present study. First, the 
evaluation used paths measured from one headphone device in one 
dummy-head wearing configuration and was conducted offline to iso­
late the effects of secondary-path delay and driver-band allocation under 
controlled linear conditions. Accordingly, the virtual electronic delay 
discussed in Section 4.4 should be interpreted as a controlled paramet­
ric perturbation rather than as a measured ADC/DAC implementation, 
and the present contribution should be read as an isolation study of 
delay-related mechanisms rather than as a replacement for real-time im­
plementation studies. The comparison in Table 2 should be interpreted 
in the same way: it reports architecture-level control-output arithmetic, 
coefficient-vector commitment schedule, and coefficient storage, not 
a complete adaptation-operation count or a power analysis for head­
phone hardware. Measured execution-time evidence is available only 
at the indirect platform level reported in Appendix C; the exact config­
urations evaluated here were not implemented and timed directly. In 
particular, sample-by-sample anti-noise generation and coefficient com­
mitment every 𝐵 samples have different latency implications. Increasing 
𝐵 reduces the commitment event rate and aggregate coefficient-element 
update rate but slows convergence and tracking. Power consumption 
was not estimated because it depends on the target processor, its 
clock and voltage settings, numerical precision, memory access, com­
piler optimization, and implementation-specific scheduling; a MAC-rate 
calculation alone does not provide a defensible platform-level power 
value. A real implementation would additionally include secondary-
path modeling error, microphone noise, finite processing precision, 
input–output scheduling, controller timing for the exact configurations 
evaluated here, and wearing-position variation. Loudspeaker nonlin­
earity and the coupled influence of secondary-to-reference acoustic 
feedback, discussed below, are also outside the validated scope. These 
factors can alter convergence, stability, and the attained control solution 
and are not represented by the present idealized linear measured-path
evaluation.
Second, loudspeaker nonlinearities were not included. The woofer 
has a fundamental resonance near 150 Hz, and a large low-frequency 
drive in a physical device may approach excursion limits and generate 
distortion that is not represented in the linear measured-path model. 
Similarly, the low-frequency values obtained under tweeter-only bypass 
operation should not be interpreted as evidence that such operation is 
physically desirable, as driving the tweeter below its efficient range can 
increase distortion and instability risk. This is why the bypass conditions 
are treated as delay references rather than practical recommendations.
Third, the secondary-to-reference acoustic paths were excluded from 
the FxNLMS adaptation. The path-level measurements in Appendix A 
retain the approximately 11 dB local minimum separation reported in 
the original analysis, but this path ratio alone does not account for the 
control-output level or the primary-reference signal. The added post-
hoc signal-level analysis addresses this limitation by comparing the 
estimated secondary-induced component with the primary-reference sig­
nal over 100–4000 Hz. Across all nine conditions and three reference 
microphones, the largest L/R-averaged ratio was −20.77 dB, and the 
largest mean across the three reference microphones was −22.40 dB. The 
largest single-ear value was −19.88 dB. These values quantitatively sup­
port the uncoupled modeling approximation under the present paths, 
input signals, and converged control outputs, but they do not show 
that acoustic feedback is uniformly negligible or that it cannot af­
fect adaptation. Because the control outputs used in the estimate were 
obtained without feedback coupling, the analysis does not allow the 
feedback component to alter the reference signals, filtered-reference cor­
relations, filter coefficients, or convergence behavior. It is therefore not 
a coupled-path validation. Feedback cancellation based on identified 
secondary-to-reference paths is a possible implementation measure, but 
it was not evaluated in this study.
Finally, Appendix D varies the cutoff frequency, common step size, 
and gain-matching band one factor at a time. The results show that the 
single-filter bypass/high-pass condition is insensitive to the tested cut­
off frequencies and to gain-matching bands ending at or below 2 kHz, 
whereas the dual-filter foreground/background ranking is more cutoff-
dependent and the high-frequency-inclusive gain-matching stress cases 
reduce the single-filter performance. The common-step-size sweep also 
identifies non-attenuating dual-filter cases at the larger tested values. 
These discrete sweeps do not constitute joint optimization. Filter or­
der, separate woofer and tweeter step sizes, and interactions among 
the parameters were not evaluated. Different driver units, enclosure ge­
ometries, wearing conditions, or target noise spectra may therefore shift 
the preferred allocation and parameter values. The contribution of this 
paper is not a universal audio-crossover design rule, but rather a delay-
minimizing driver-allocation principle for causality-limited broadband 
feedforward ANC in open-ear headphones. The representative delay 
budget in Table 5 should likewise be interpreted as a design aid for 
this allocation, not as a measured end-to-end delay analysis or an ex­
act component-wise decomposition of arbitrary worn-device secondary 
paths.
6 . Conclusion
This paper presented a controlled, idealized linear measured-path 
offline study of a wideband feedforward ANC design using one open-
ear headphone device in one dummy-head wearing configuration. 
The design uses a design-oriented secondary-path delay budget for 
driver-band allocation. We introduced a coaxially offset 2-way sec­
ondary source, in which the woofer is utilized for low-frequency 
control owing to its smaller mechanical delay and the tweeter is 
utilized for higher-frequency control owing to its shorter acoustic 
path. Two implementations were formulated: a dual-filter configura­
tion with independent adaptive-filter branches and both background 
and foreground band-limiting placements, and a single-filter config­
uration with a single adaptive-filter branch and a fixed crossover
network.
Isolated driver measurements confirmed the intended mechanical-
delay complementarity: the woofer is more favorable below approxi­
mately 500 Hz, while the tweeter becomes mechanically competitive 
at higher frequencies. Under this measured-path condition and at the 
nominal controlled virtual electronic delay of 104 µs, the proposed two-
driver configurations increased the band-limited noise-power reduction 
for broadband pink noise relative to the single-driver baselines. From 
100Hz to2000Hz, Du-BpHp-Bg and Xo-BpHp achieved 8.01 and 7.58 dB, 
respectively, compared with 5.94 dB for Wf-Bp and 5.02 dB for Tw-Bp. 
The delay sweep also showed that the two-driver advantage is most pro­
nounced in the low-to-mid-frequency range when the electronic delay 
is small, which is consistent with the causality-based design rationale of 
the paper.
Under the tested conditions, these results support delay-oriented 
driver allocation as a design approach for causality-limited broad­
band feedforward ANC in open-ear headphones. For a new device, 
driver and filter selection should first balance the measured ampli­
tude response and mechanical group delay of each driver with the 
source-to-ear acoustic offset, crossover-filter group delay, and avail­
able electronic-delay budget. The architecture should then be selected 
according to whether preserving the output-path causality margin or 
reducing foreground computation and coefficient storage is the domi­
nant constraint. Future work will include evaluation under nonlinear
